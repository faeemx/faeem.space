We live in a culture obsessed with scale. The prevailing narrative in software tells us that if an idea cannot reach millions of users, raise venture capital, or generate recurring revenue, it isn't worth building.

I think this obsession with scale has robbed programming of much of its intimacy and joy.

Some of the most satisfying things I have ever made were small, quirky software tools intended for an audience of exactly one person: myself.

## The Freedom of Zero Stakeholders

When you build something for public consumption or commercial viability, you are immediately bound by compromise:
- You must write onboarding flows for people who don't understand your domain.
- You must design generic configuration menus to accommodate every possible edge case.
- You have to write authentication, password resets, and privacy policies.

But when you build for yourself, all of that baggage evaporates. 

If you prefer your date format as `YYYY.MM.DD`, you simply hardcode it. If you want the app to behave differently on Sundays, you write three lines of logic and move on. There is no backlog grooming, no roadmap debate, and no need to justify your aesthetic preferences to a committee.

## Digital Whittling

I like to think of personal software as modern woodcarving or digital whittling. 

A carpenter who carves a wooden spoon for their kitchen drawer isn't thinking about mass manufacturing in a factory. They are enjoying the tactile grain of the cedar, the sharp bite of the gouge, and the quiet satisfaction of using a tool made by their own hands each morning at breakfast.

Software can be that, too. 

A little terminal script that counts down the steep time of your morning oolong tea. A simple photo organizer that tags images by the notebook journal they belong to. A minimal website like this one—built without trackers, without ads, and without growth hacking.

## The Joy of the Craft

Programming began as a deeply personal craft—a creative outlet where human thought met the logic of silicon.

If you are feeling burnt out by tickets, sprints, and corporate metrics, try building something small this weekend that nobody else will ever see. You might be surprised at how quickly the joy comes rushing back.
